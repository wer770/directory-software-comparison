## **What Directory Software Is Supposed to Do (and Why Most of It Doesn't)**

A directory website is, at its core, a searchable collection of listings grouped by category, location, or topic. Users come to find. Listers come to be found. You, the operator, sit in the middle and monetize the connection.

That sounds simple until you try to build it. Real directory software has to handle, at minimum:

- Front-end listing submission so business owners add and manage their own listings without touching your admin panel
- Custom fields per category (a restaurant needs different fields than a law firm)
- Google Maps integration with location-aware search and filtering
- Paid listing monetization — one-time fees, recurring subscriptions, featured placements, claim-listing workflows
- Reviews and ratings tied to listings
- SEO-friendly URLs, schema markup, and indexable category/location pages
- Member management, gated content, and permissions
- Email campaigns and member communication
- Hosting, backups, and uptime — because your directory being down means your members' phones stop ringing

If a platform is missing more than two or three of those, it isn't directory software. It's a contact list with ambitions.

## **The Real Architectural Question: Self-Hosted or SaaS?**

Before comparing specific products, you have to answer one question honestly: do you want to **own** your directory, or **rent** it?

**Self-hosted (WordPress + a directory plugin)** means you install WordPress on hosting you control, add a plugin like GeoDirectory or Directorist, and your data, design, and customer relationships live on your server. You own the equity. You also own the maintenance — security updates, hosting bills, the small technical work WordPress demands.

**SaaS (Brilliant Directories, eDirectory, Wild Apricot)** means you pay a monthly fee and your site runs on the vendor's infrastructure with their templates, their feature set, their support. Faster setup. Less technical work. But your data lives on their servers, your design flexibility is capped at what they allow, and the recurring fee compounds — $149/month is $1,788/year, every year, forever.

There's no universally right answer. Self-hosted wins on long-term cost and ownership. SaaS wins on speed-to-launch and "I don't want to think about server updates." The honest comparison is which trade-off matches your situation, not which one is morally superior.
